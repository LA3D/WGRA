What LLMs actually do with a research wiki

retrieval
telemetry
We keep a wiki as an agent’s durable memory. Reading the transcripts back, we measured how the agent really uses it across three research wikis. It reads once and reuses from context, greps far more than it reads whole pages, and barely follows the links you wrote for it.
Author

Chris Sweet

Published

July 24, 2026

A wiki-as-memory is only worth the trouble if the agent leans on it, and “leans on it” is easy to assume and hard to see. So we built a small tool that reconstructs every wiki touch from a session’s raw transcript, and pointed it at three research wikis. The behaviour is more specific, and more lopsided, than the pitch.

The instrument

An agent reaches a wiki three ways that a naive “count the tool calls” scan would split or miss: the Read tool, Bash grep/cat, and git (writing the wiki). The tool unifies all three, then asks, per user question, whether the answer was wiki-informed and by what route, a fresh fetch, reuse of a page an earlier turn already pulled into context, or the index injected at session start.

Science reads, dev commits

The first split is in why each project touches its wiki. The research wiki (markov) spends most of its wiki interaction reading; the code project (yarp) spends most of it committing. And when the researcher retrieves, it reads whole pages; the developer greps for fragments.

markov 36 : 6 55% 10% 34%
yarp 22 : 24 24% 60% 31%

Read once, cite many

Across all three, the answer is wiki-informed on roughly a third of questions, and the dominant route is neither a fresh fetch nor the injected index. It is reuse: a page an earlier turn already read, still sitting in context, informing a later answer. That “still in context” is not a guess. Chronologically, a page read earlier stays in the model’s window, it is literally cached, and only a session compaction can evict it, which touches just a handful of these reused answers. Reuse is the model working from its cache rather than re-deriving. The single sharpest number is the re-read rate, how often the agent re-fetches a page it has already seen instead of reusing it: 10% in markov (read once, then reuse) versus 60% in yarp (re-greps the same pages over and over). Same axis again, the researcher treats the wiki as memory, the developer treats it as search.

And the wiki’s fingerprint on answers is bigger than a citation count would show. If you count answers that reproduce a verbatim phrase from a page body, not just those that name a page, the wiki-informed count climbs sharply: in yarp, from 19 answers that named a page to 47 that quoted one, and markov (12 to 18) and vision (57 to 87) show the same jump. The agent routinely quotes a page it never cites. And since the SessionStart hook injects page titles, not bodies, that quoted text can only have come from a page it actually read, so the wiki is genuinely feeding answers, not merely being name-dropped.

Hub-and-search, and the index carries the traffic

The map below is the observed wiki: each node is a page, each arrow a jump the agent actually made from one page to the next inside a single answer. A solid arrow means the destination was named on the source page (a followed link); a dashed arrow means the jump came from a grep, the index, or the question instead. Fewer than half of all jumps are solid, so navigation is mostly name-matching and hub-and-search rather than link-walking. Drag any node to explore.

followed a named link grep / index / question jump ● node size = times the page was read · blue = index/log hub · drag any node

That blue index/log hub at the centre of the wheel is the second finding: the agent orbits the navigation pages. Between 43% and 67% of all page-to-page jumps pass through an index, log, or home page, it reads a content page, returns to the index, and spokes back out. The most central, load-bearing nodes in the observed wiki are exactly the ones you rarely think of as content: the index and the log.

One thing this map cannot tell you, and it is worth being honest about: whether the model walks the graph you author, the related:/up: typed edges, or just the links in the prose. The wiki mirrors 89 to 99% of its typed edges into the body, so almost every followed link sits in both places at once, and nothing in the transcript records which representation the model’s attention used. The one unambiguous case, a page reachable only through a frontmatter edge, barely exists (1 to 11% of authored edges) and is followed essentially never. So whether the authored graph is genuinely used is confounded here; settling it takes an ablation, hold the question set fixed and strip one representation, not a passive trace like this one. What is solid: most movement is hub-and-search, and the links it does follow are ones present in the prose.

The wiki answers the questions it gets

Most turns are not questions at all: they are commands, edits, and running jobs. Segment those out and look only at genuine knowledge questions, and the wiki-informed rate rises, most of all for the dev project: 60% of yarp’s knowledge questions were wiki-informed, versus 31% across all its turns. Dev asks few questions (20% of turns), but the wiki serves the ones it gets, better than either research wiki. It was never that the wiki failed dev; dev just spends most of its time executing.

What this is, and isn’t

This is a measure of behaviour, not value. It shows how the wiki is accessed, not whether it made an answer better, which is a counterfactual a transcript simply cannot show. But the behaviour is itself the finding: the observed usage graph is the empirical baseline that any smarter retrieval, for instance a distribution-based Markov walk over the wiki’s structure, has to beat. Today the agent leans hard on reused context and hub-and-search, and barely on the link graph you wrote for it. That gap is the opportunity.

Honest caveats. Three wikis, one (long) session each, so treat the rates as indicative, not settled. A session that is itself about wikis inflates the reuse numbers. Bash parsing is heuristic, and the model stores only a replay signature for its private reasoning, so “why this page” is inferred from structure, not read from the model’s own words.
